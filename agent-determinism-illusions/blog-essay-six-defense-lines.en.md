# A Reviewer Nailed Me in Six Places

**Or: You admitted the gap — and may still be fleeing**

*2026-07-13*

---

Version 3. A position piece. No scores. No model tuning. Trust staked entirely on argument.

I was even pleased: no experiments, so no one could say it wouldn't reproduce. Logic was home ground. Clean.

Handed to a severe review — a model role run to a harsh standard, one structured pass, not six rounds of theater. Six points came back. I admitted it: six errors I thought only beginners make. Not a climax. A revision list. In the background, four proposed constraints (P1–P4): how dangerous samples enter, how they stay out of updates. Proposals. Not proven theorems. Almost every red line sat where "proposal" had been written as "discovery."

---

"This paper has not proven irreducibility. An honest gap." I thought: sincere enough; they will keep reading.

The point: you admitted it, and still wrote in the voice of "I discovered." Honesty does not waive burden of proof. Readers don't feel truth — they feel a shield. First reflex: that wasn't my intent. The behavior was. Admission that does not reroute the argument is pose. Swap the shield for a promise: here are the ways to catch me.

---

A smooth counterexample: a dangerous trajectory "surprises" the model, gets lifted into training, so some reweighting is unsafe. The point: what if it doesn't surprise? Pull the condition, force goes to zero. You attacked a phenomenon; attack the mechanism — the system never asks "may this be learned?" Change the scene, dodge the story; you cannot dodge "no door was built." Force is not "you were wrong." Force is "you didn't do it."

---

I once drew a dataflow with Greek letters, leaned back: this is a paper with math. The point was short: not formalization — a flowchart wearing symbols. If you can say input X → output Y, it starts to look like if-else; if you can say it and still cannot render pseudocode, it is mostly décor. Three lines of admission replaced the ornamental chain. Symbol count does not save you. Decidability does.

---

In a dependency table, a hard line: drop P4 → still the old method. Split by questions: do the earlier clauses remain? Does the old method have them? No answer. A falsehood. Intent was "incomplete"; hunger for impact wrote "identical." One false line makes readers doubt every true one. Better admit weakness than pretend return to origin.

---

Half a rule parked in "future work": isolation must keep analytic value — hard to quantify; the main protocol only tested "not entering training." The point: an isolation zone can fill and never be read, and still satisfy the letter. You called it core, and checked half. The floor must enter the protocol: if isolated, at least seen. Whether seeing helps is future work. Moving what must be checked is fleeing the check.

---

Failure conditions named 90%, 10%, K≥3. Why not 80%? No answer. Written casually, treated as iron in the body. The problem is not having numbers; it is that someone running the protocol cannot tell whether changing a threshold breaks the contract. Mark placeholders; fix them at preregistration; block the after-the-fact "this would also do." Casual writing is allowed. Costume it as rule without a label, not.

---

After six fixes, the content did not change in essence. What changed was armor: whether claim and burden aligned. Same border as the series — text can perform compliance; after idea loses its referee, guard harder against "sounds like"; the floor must not fall back to self-report. Zero experiments push the border until only whether the argument can be tested remains.

One question: if the reviewer draws the line here, can I defend without changing a word?

If not, you are still on the inner side of the cliff, thinking you stand. If yes, then half a step forward is discussable.

If you finish thinking "six useful rules," save them as a writing checklist, then in the next position piece use "honest gap" again to block pursuit — the nails remain. Only now you sit on them yourself.
